# The Manager, the self, and governing symbolic entities

24 November 2026 · Research note following the 23 November discussion

## 1. Finding

The November discussion makes explicit a distinction that Manager Theory needs. The **Manager** is the human stewardship function. A **governing symbolic entity** is an instantiated pattern that can direct that function by shaping the criteria it uses to evaluate futures. The **self** is provisionally the lived and represented continuity of the person whose capacities, commitments, and future are at stake. These concepts refer to different aspects of one human life.

The general mechanism of symbolic enactment is already established within the research framework. The specific addition proposed here is that an enacted symbolic pattern can organize **how the Manager selects and revises its own direction**. An institutional script can supply a trajectory or restrict access to alternatives. Its enactment as a governing principle can also set the standards by which the Manager decides which alternatives deserve consideration, what counts as a cost, and which evidence warrants a change of course. This is a candidate mechanism at the level of individual stewardship. Its novelty is within Manager Theory and depends on whether it explains decisions beyond the theory’s existing accounts of constraint, valuing, and environmental mismatch.

## 3. Proposed mechanism

A pattern becomes governing in a domain when its enactment **reliably orders competing considerations across decisions**, including decisions about retaining or revising the role associated with it. A public rule may be encountered through language, routine, reward, or affect before it becomes available for explicit reflection. The receiving person’s reconstruction may preserve enough of the pattern to enact it, transform it into a different identifiable pattern, or acquire only expectations and habits from its effects. Evidence of influence alone leaves these possibilities open.

A minimal sequence is **instantiated pattern → compatible human capacities → changed criteria for evaluating possible roles → different stewardship choices**. Those choices can sustain relationships, routines, and allocations that favour later enactment, applying the October 25 feedback loop. For example, a role can direct surplus toward maintaining the conditions under which it makes sense, while leaving less for examining alternatives. That outcome is conditional on actual resources, opportunities, and competing commitments.

The same pattern can govern one domain and yield in another. Multiple patterns and bodily or relational demands can jointly shape a decision. The claim requires neither a single controlling entity nor a new component in the surplus model. To attribute governance to a symbolic entity, identify a reproducible pattern and a plausible route of enactment, then establish that the pattern changes evaluation. Labelling any persistent preference or unexamined habit a symbolic entity would make the claim circular.

## 4. Consequences for role selection, survival, and self-direction

### I. Prospective role selection

A possible role acts in the present through its current representation. The Manager encounters roles through the paths it can see, the capabilities and support it can mobilize, and the criteria by which it recognizes a future as worth pursuing. An inherited role can therefore function both as an available trajectory and as a standard for judging other trajectories. The second possibility explains how a role can remain authoritative after its external enforcement weakens. It also leaves room for the same role to be deliberately renewed after examination.

A prolonged garden experience can reflect viable reliance on ongoing support, mistaken expectations about how long it will last, or a commitment that makes continuing the protected mode of life especially important. The first two already follow from the Garden and delegation accounts. The third belongs to the governance proposition only if a reproducible pattern can be identified as ordering the decision.

Talent is durable capability in the existing terminology. It contributes to surplus when available and unclaimed. A Manager may overestimate what others can do independently by treating its own unusual capability or access to support as typical. That is a problem of estimating heterogeneous capability and its prerequisites. It becomes relevant to symbolic governance when an enacted pattern systematically determines whose competence is recognized or which forms of dependence count as legitimate.

### II. Survival

Biological viability remains the starting requirement of stewardship and a material limit on continued enactment. It does not guarantee priority in every decision. A governing pattern can support survival by organizing foresight, reciprocity, and maintenance. It can also direct effort toward the continuity of a role or commitment while putting bodily upkeep or the capacity to adapt at risk.

The inference is strongest when the alternative is actually feasible for this person and there is evidence that the risk and alternative entered the Manager’s deliberation but were subordinated to the governing criterion. An observer’s view that a survival strategy was obvious leaves open whether the Manager could see it, enact it with available resources, or accept its relational consequences. Acknowledged, deliberate risk for a commitment likewise provides no basis by itself for diagnosing a failure of self-direction.

### III. Self-direction

The working use of **self** here holds together lived continuity and the person’s representation of who they are. Their relationship remains open. An articulated identity can carry symbolic patterns, and a role can be experienced as part of who one is. The demand to “be myself” can express an examined commitment, an inherited role experienced as indispensable, or a response to bodily, affective, and relational realities. Its wording alone cannot decide among them.

A provisional criterion for self-direction is the person’s practical capacity, exercised through the Manager, to examine the commitments organizing their choices, trace their dependencies and costs, and retain or revise them in light of experience. This can include endorsing an inherited direction. Examination requires available capacity and legible alternatives, as the existing account of surplus and Grace already suggests. The additional question is whether the governing pattern determines which commitments may be examined at all. This account needs no second supervisor behind the Manager.

## 5. What would earn the extension

The distinguishing case would show a change in **how the same options are evaluated** after an identifiable pattern is adopted, reinterpreted, or loses authority, while actual capabilities, support, external sanctions, and feasible options remain sufficiently comparable. Evidence could include repeated choices and contemporaneous records showing which considerations counted, supplemented by observed changes after the pattern was questioned or differently enacted. Comparisons should also track hidden obligations, information, and loss of support. A single declaration of identity or a single refusal cannot establish the mechanism.

If the choice tracks a change in external penalties, credible support, available capacity, or the accuracy of environmental expectations, the existing accounts already have explanatory work to do. If an identifiable pattern also changes how the Manager judges those facts and what could justify revision, the proposed distinction adds explanatory value. If existing accounts of valuing already specify that relationship, the gain is a clarification of their application to the Manager’s own direction.

## 6. Unresolved questions

1. What structural continuity identifies the same entity across internal reconstructions and indirect transmission through an environment?
2. When does influence on a judgment become governance across a domain, and how do conflicting commitments gain or lose priority?
3. How can a study distinguish revised standards of evaluation from revised beliefs about the consequences of leaving a role?
4. What lets a Manager examine the criteria by which it decides what merits examination? How much depends on surplus, access to other perspectives, or the ability to tolerate a change in identity?
5. How should lived continuity, explicit self-representation, and reflective endorsement figure in a fuller account of the self?
