## 2. Episode-level symbolic constellations

The biological Manager is a *host for enactment*, not automatically a sovereign self deciding every goal. A symbolic entity is an instantiated pattern that requires compatible physical and social capacities to produce effects. The self, to the extent that it can be distinguished, must not be installed as an otherwise unexplained controller.

A person inherits or learns a large symbolic repertoire from evolutionary and civilizational history, institutions, groups, and individual experience. Only some patterns are active in a given episode. They may organize attention, role expectations, acceptable actions, allocation of effort, interpretation, and the criteria by which alternatives are judged.

**Working description:** an *active symbolic constellation* is an observer's description of simultaneously enacted, partially overlapping patterns within a host during an episode. It is **not automatically another symbolic entity**. To identify an additional higher-order entity, one would need to show a reproducible, instantiated structure with relevant persistence and a compatible path of enactment.

Properties worth observing:

1. **Duration:** an enactment may last moments, span an assignment, or persist across years.
2. **Co-activation:** patterns may complement, compete for scarce attention or resources, directly conflict, or regulate the enactment of other patterns.
3. **Priority changes:** the same Manager can enact a protector role in one episode, a rebel role in another, and both simultaneously where different obligations are concerned.
4. **Nested scales:** local practices may stabilize into institutional rules; inherited institutions may constrain local practice. These are already examples of accumulated coherence and mediated top-down feedback.
5. **Partial observability:** the host's behavior does not unambiguously reveal which entities, expectations, sanctions, or bodily requirements produced it.

This operational account does not require a permanent psychological class for each person, a second hidden supervisor, or an additional formal parameter set.

## 3. Social mediation of survival during development

A developing human is maintained within a Garden of Eden provided by other Managers. The child initially meets physiological needs through a social system of provisioning and protection, then progressively acquires stewardship and room for independent enactment. Other mammals and birds offer comparative examples of protected, staged learning, without establishing identical symbolic mechanisms.

Consequently, some social rules are learned as conditions governing access to protection, resources, belonging, or recognized capability. Their *stated reason*, their *actual operating consequence*, and the *objective of the person enforcing them* may differ. Rules can be enacted before the young Manager understands their origin.

The existing Garden note already describes progressive transfer of stewardship. What matters for the present topic is that a familiar survival response can later be activated by a socially learned requirement, even if the rule's original function or the environment has changed.

A continued demand for garden-like support, failure to update environmental expectations, and persistence of inherited roles are already alternative explanations in the [Manager/Self note](2026-11-24-manager-self-and-governing-symbolic-entities.md). So is talent: an unusual capability may create real surplus while distorting a Manager's estimates of what others can perceive or do. These are **not** evidence by themselves that a distinct symbolic entity has taken control.

## 4. Recurring social functions and archetypal roles

Human groups repeatedly face provisioning, care, protection, education, coordination, exploration, disagreement, and adaptation. Repeated responses can become culturally recognizable figures such as caregiver, protector, teacher, mediator, explorer, rebel, or renewer.

The minimal analytical sequence is:

**Recurring group requirement → recurring functional response → recognizable narrative or role pattern → possible encoded and transmissible symbolic entity.**

This is a possible explanation for the recurrence of archetypal figures; it does **not** require adopting Jung's collective-unconscious account, regarding archetypes as inherited complete programs, or treating every role label as a symbolic entity. The role's symbolic status depends on the existing instantiation and compatibility criteria.

A role is not the same as a personality, and observed behavior does not determine that a manager consciously selected the role. Furthermore:

- A **rebel** opposes, violates, or contests an established pattern.
- A **renewer** helps produce, test, or stabilize an alternative.
- A single person may do both, either, or neither. Rule-breaking is not sufficient proof of adaptive correction.
- A culturally recognized rebel may itself become a prescribed playbook; apparent rebellion against one rule may enact another established rule.

## 5. Vantage points, tacit objectives, and interacting Managers

Managers may act within one shared material situation but possess different capabilities, histories, commitments, information, and dependencies. The apparent common event is therefore filtered through different possibilities and perceived costs.

Distinguish at least four things:

1. **Publicly stated rule or justification:** the explanation available to a broader audience.
2. **Operative function:** what the rule or practice does to capabilities, access, power, and shared conditions.
3. **Situated objective:** what a participant or group is trying to accomplish, including objectives that may be tacit, private, or strategically concealed.
4. **Observer's interpretation:** an inference from the visible action, with no guarantee of access to its governing pattern.

Concealment is possible; it must not be inferred merely because a stated explanation and observed effect diverge. Unnoticed dependencies, obsolete justifications, inadequate information, or different time horizons are competing explanations.

The existing single-Manager Event → Filter → Pressure → Action → Changed Event loop can be considered across multiple Managers, each with its own partial perspective. Enactments alter the same shared environment; resulting consequences are then encountered and interpreted differently. Cooperation, negotiated dependencies, signaling, conflict, and misrecognition may follow. The functional labels *protector*, *rebel*, or *mediator* may describe the enacted position relative to another actor; they need not identify the actor's enduring self.

**New clarification:** conflict can be a struggle not only over which action should occur, but over **which interpretation of the event and which criteria for evaluating it are permitted to govern**.

## 6. Surplus maintenance, correction, and freedom as non-domination

A system must invest enough of its available capacity into maintaining the conditions that make its future operation possible. Existing surplus research already covers hidden maintenance, delayed deterioration, capacity loss, and costs shifted to other participants or generations.

When routines or institutions responsible for correction become committed to a failing allocation, a nonconforming Manager may notice and attempt an alternative. The **need for a corrective pathway** is more general than the need for a heroic rebel. Audit, ordinary reform, negotiation, technical criticism, exit, and refusal may all perform parts of that function.

**Conditional proposition:** maintenance neglect can increase pressure for a challenge to the established symbolic order, especially when ordinary correction is blocked. The direction is not a universal law: some systems suppress information, some adapt institutionally, and some deteriorate without effective dissent. A rebel can be mistaken or destructive; the value of the intervention must be assessed by consequences and the viability of the alternatives, not by the role's name.

Republican freedom as **non-domination** provides a relevant organizational perspective. A higher-order system cannot know all its members' situations, capabilities, and possible futures; governing them by an uncontestable interpretation risks destroying information and autonomy useful for its own correction. Non-arbitrary, accountable, contestable decision-making can preserve space for divergent knowledge and action. This is a potential *functional connection* to adaptation, **not** a reduction of freedom to economic efficiency or a proof that dissent is always beneficial.

A possible failure loop is:

**Neglected maintenance → falling available capacity → narrower scope for examination and dissent → weaker correction → further maintenance neglect.**

Its occurrence depends on institutions, reserves, coercive power, external support, information, and the ability to act. It should be tested, not presumed. The counter-loop is also possible: a system invests surplus in maintenance, legibility, and protected contestation, allowing errors to be identified and repaired before they become existential.

## 7. Discriminating observations and simulation candidates

These questions test whether anything here goes beyond the mechanisms already in the two research programs:

1. **Constellation versus fixed traits.** Under matched observable conditions, does the same person exhibit different combinations or priorities of reproducible role patterns across episodes? Can that be explained by changes in capability, sanction, or expectation alone?
2. **Reproducible role versus analytic label.** Is the identified protector/rebel/renewer pattern actually encoded and transmitted across carriers, or merely a researcher's classification of actions?
3. **Unequal vantage points.** Can differing information, dependencies, and talents predict divergent enactments in response to the same event without assigning different permanent personalities?
4. **Public narration versus operative objective.** Is there independent evidence of tacit or concealed coordination? What simpler interpretations fit when it is absent?
5. **Maintenance and dissent.** Holding initial capability and shocks comparable, does retaining contestable correction improve long-horizon functional capacity compared with suppressing it? Under what costs does contestation instead reduce viability?
6. **Role succession.** Can a rebel's rejection of a failing rule be distinguished from the renewer's work of constructing and maintaining an alternative?
7. **Governance of criteria.** After a reproducible pattern gains or loses authority, do standards for evaluating the *same feasible options* change, as proposed in the [Manager/Self note](2026-11-24-manager-self-and-governing-symbolic-entities.md)?

A multi-Manager simulation may represent independent repertoires, incomplete observation, and interacting enactments using existing fast/slow activation, available surplus, weighted relations, delayed effects, and maintenance mechanisms from [surplus-sim1](https://github.com/carstenbund/surplus-sim1). Do not add parameters merely to narrate examples; add them only if an identifiable causal distinction cannot be represented with existing machinery.

## 8. Limits and deferred branch

- **Urgency** remains separate: maintenance deficit, corrective pressure, and time left before irreversible loss are not equivalent. No general urgency function is proposed here.
- **The self** remains a research problem, not a new central governor. Biological hosting, autobiographical continuity, reflective endorsement, and enacted symbolic direction need not coincide.
- **Functional recurrence** is not proof of innate Jungian archetypes; recurrence may reflect shared constraints, cultural transmission, institutions, or other mechanisms.
- **Causal correction** does not automatically imply moral or political legitimacy. Normative judgment needs separate treatment of agency, harm, power, and alternatives.
- **Symbolic persistence** does not imply host viability. A pattern may continue to be enacted even while consuming the host's maintenance capacity.

## 9. Research contribution

The synthesis is primarily a **mapping between established mechanisms**. The substantive additions worth testing are: (a) episode-level co-activation and changing priority of enacted patterns; (b) role and conflict as perspectival rather than intrinsic classifications; (c) separation of public narration, operative function, and participant objectives; and (d) contestable correction as one potential use of surplus supporting a system's capacity to maintain itself.

These extend the **operational analysis** of Manager Theory and symbolic enactment, without changing the symbolic entity definition, introducing a new universal archetype taxonomy, or claiming a new law of survival.
